Objectives & Problem Statement

Why this project, and what it set out to answer

A large share of fresh produce is thrown away because spoilage is noticed too late. This project asks whether combining three kinds of sensing catches it more reliably, and whether such a system survives the sensor failures real storage rooms have.

The problem

Deciding whether produce is still good relies mostly on sight: colour, spots, visible softness. Manual inspection is subjective, slow and does not scale to warehouse volumes, and by the time spoilage is visible it is often too late for a distributor or retailer to act.

Spoilage is a multi-signal process. The surface changes colour and texture; surface temperature shifts as respiration and microbial activity change, sometimes before anything is visible; and decaying produce releases gases such as methane. A thermal camera and a gas sensor can pick up the second and third of these, which a photograph cannot.

Problem statement

Given a sequence of daily observations of a fruit or vegetable, where each day may have an sRGB image, a thermal image and methane readings, and any of them may be missing, predict whether the produce is spoiled and estimate how many days it has left.

The gap in existing work

Most published freshness classifiers use colour images alone. A few recent systems combine two sensors, but each targets a single food or a different task, and none tests what happens when a sensor fails.

No earlier system combines visual, thermal and gas sensing, trains one model across several produce types, or treats sensor failure as something to design for and test.

Objectives

  1. 01

    Fuse three sensors in one model

    Combine sRGB images, thermal images and methane readings across six produce types with cross-modal attention and a temporal model over days.

  2. 02

    Handle missing data inside the model

    Use GRU-D for irregular gas readings, and leave a missing sensor out of fusion instead of filling it with a made-up value.

  3. 03

    Learn three tasks without hand-tuned weights

    Train spoilage classification, days-until-spoilage regression and gas reconstruction together with an uncertainty-weighted loss.

  4. 04

    Evaluate honestly on a tiny dataset

    Use seed ensembles, calibrated thresholds, bootstrap confidence intervals, significance tests and multiple-comparison correction, and keep the test set out of every decision.

  5. 05

    Measure robustness to sensor failure

    Switch each sensor off on up to half of the days and compare against simpler early and late fusion baselines.

  6. 06

    Report what does not work

    Run ablation, backbone, cross-produce, interpretability and regression analyses, and publish negative results with the same care as positive ones.

Why it matters

In 2022 an estimated 1.05 billion tonnes of food were wasted at retail, food-service and household level, 19% of the food available to consumers. Catching spoilage earlier supports UN Sustainable Development Goal 12.3, which calls for halving food waste and reducing post-harvest losses, and indirectly SDG 2, Zero Hunger.
